Interim findings

From recorded communication to useful feedback

This project investigates two stages of communication feedback in paediatric virtual-reality training. Charlie examines whether acoustic evidence can help interpret behaviours within an established seven-category rubric. Kang Zhe examines how existing assessment worksheets can become useful learner and instructor reports. Speech experiments demonstrate reproducible acoustic measurement and prediction on external datasets, while clinical demonstrations expose attribution and interpretation problems. A deterministic feedback prototype establishes repeatable extraction and report generation while exposing gaps in coverage, interpretation and educational specificity. Together, the findings identify what must be verified before additional evidence or redesigned feedback can support training. The prototypes remain separate; neither has demonstrated improved clinical communication or learning.

01 · Shared context

Problem and contribution

Paediatric resuscitation requires teams to allocate roles, exchange instructions and update a shared understanding under time pressure. In a hypothetical equipment request, a transcript may preserve the words while omitting delivery and overlapping speech; a worksheet may identify a communication problem while leaving trainees unsure how to improve. These are distinct gaps between recorded speech, assessment and learning.

VR-405 builds on the NUS Immersive Reality Lab’s multi-user simulation and prior clinical collaboration. The upstream research team developed speaker-attributed transcription, discourse-thread reconstruction and evidence-linked positive/negative assessment worksheets. Its manuscript describes seven categories refined with clinical subject-matter experts: Roles & Responsibilities, Closed Loop Communication, Clear Messages, Knowing One’s Limitations, Constructive Intervention, Summarisation & Re-evaluation, and Knowledge Sharing. These categories and the upstream system are inherited work, not capstone inventions. [P1]

The capstone addresses two questions the existing assessment does not settle: whether vocal evidence helps interpret an established behaviour, and whether a different presentation helps readers use the assessment. Published paediatric VR research supports investigating debriefing in this setting, but does not validate our methods. Sohlin et al.’s 24-professional pilot found the training feasible; a larger study was needed to evaluate non-inferiority between debriefing methods. [1]

Value and intended deliverables. The project aims to test whether audio helps reviewers apply the existing rubric and to develop feedback formats that connect assessed behaviour to a defensible next action. Deliverables are a bounded speech-evidence protocol and feasibility findings (Charlie), and a traceable report generator with fidelity and usefulness evaluation (Kang Zhe). The research value is in establishing which evidence and feedback readers can use in this setting.

02 · Charlie Hee Ming Guan

What can audio add to communication assessment?

From vocal impressions to a rubric question

Charlie’s initial work explored perceived vocal confidence: whether acoustic measurements could recover how certain a speaker sounded. Listening to selected existing simulation recordings also drew attention to laughter during a positively assessed summary and hesitant delivery during a recognised self-correction. These observations prompted a proposal for additional Professionalism and Confidence columns. Examiner feedback challenged that approach because the upstream project already defines seven communication categories.

The revised question is whether correctly attributed audio helps reviewers identify or interpret an existing rubric behaviour compared with transcript-only review. This changes the purpose of the acoustic work: a detectable vocal pattern needs a defensible relationship to an assessed behaviour before it can contribute to scoring. The exploratory studies below address three supporting questions: can acoustic features predict listener ratings, can a detector locate events for review, and how do production measurements relate to listener understanding? Their results guide the revised study; they do not retrospectively test its hypothesis.

The manuscript defines Clear Messages through directedness, specificity and brevity, including the referent, action and timing. Closed Loop Communication requires a directed request, acknowledgement stating the intended action, and completion or confirmation. [P1] Audio could help recover an acknowledgement obscured by overlapping speech or distinguish which speaker responded. That would improve the evidence available for an existing criterion. By contrast, a confident tone cannot supply a missing acknowledgement, and laughter alone cannot establish a communication failure.

These are candidate uses to investigate, not observations already verified in our recordings. An equipment request with an unspecified receiver still lacks that information unless the surrounding exchange establishes it; vocal emphasis alone does not settle directedness. The first study therefore needs a rubric-owner-approved criterion and explicit rules for speaker attribution and incomplete evidence.

Testing vocal impressions and their limits

Reading confidence. An available labelled corpus allowed us to test the acoustic pipeline before collecting local ratings. It contains 600 children’s reading recordings rated by teachers. We extracted 88 eGeMAPSv02 summaries plus duration and three RMS amplitude descriptors, then compared a majority baseline, logistic regression and Random Forest using five stratified recording-level folds. Imputation and logistic scaling were fitted within each training fold. Macro-F1, which averages class-specific F1 scores equally, was 0.1916, 0.7104 and 0.7849, respectively. [3] [4]

Matched-fold ablations tested whether the result relied on simple recording properties. Logistic macro-F1 was 0.7262 with eGeMAPS alone and 0.5487 with duration alone. Duration therefore carries useful prediction information, but does not explain the full result. Acoustic descriptors can still encode speaker, text or microphone differences. An authoritative speaker map was unavailable, so the folds cannot establish performance on unseen speakers. Numeric class names are inferred from published counts rather than an explicit release legend. Ablation methods and results.

The saved logistic model supported private demonstrations. Applying it to spontaneous adult speech exposed uncertain interpretations around silence, cross-talk and missing context. This made the distinction between predicting a dataset label and assessing clinical communication concrete. RMS is a digital amplitude measure; duration includes silence and is not speaking rate.

Emotional delivery. A separate CREMA-D study examined another listener-perception target while holding speakers apart. It used 720 acted recordings from 30 speakers and four sentences, with 20/5/5 speakers assigned to training, validation and test. Logistic regression on 88 eGeMAPS features predicted the emotion receiving the most audio-only listener votes. Sixty-two tied recordings were excluded from single-label scoring.

On 113 scored test clips, macro-F1 was 0.367 versus 0.117 for the majority baseline, but accuracy was 45.1% versus 54.0%. The model recognised some minority-class examples while making more errors overall; the majority baseline always predicted neutral, which accounted for 61 test clips. Training macro-F1 of 0.886 fell sharply on unseen speakers. These results support limited class discrimination, not reliable emotion recognition. Acted delivery and shared sentences further restrict transfer. Methods, confusion matrices and implementation.

Locating events for contextual review

Laughter detection. Event retrieval offered a narrower task than interpreting an overall impression. A pretrained YAMNet pipeline processed eight original WAV channels and produced 19 candidate intervals for listening. Plausible examples were found informally, but there was no exhaustive event annotation from which to calculate precision or recall. Channels share acoustic content, and microphone identity does not establish who laughed. The useful output at this stage is a review location, not an assessment judgement. Private-demo provenance.

Separating speech production from listener understanding

Intelligibility offered an observable listener outcome to examine alongside vocal impressions. Clear-speech research shows that speakers can change several acoustic dimensions without simply slowing down. [2] The Edinburgh analysis asked which production changes our measurements could detect; it did not measure listener understanding.

The corpus contained 125 recordings from one speaker: 25 matched sentences in five conditions. Relative to plain speech, first-to-last non-pure-silence annotation spans increased in all 25 computer-directed pairs (median +1.73 s) and all 25 non-native-listener-directed pairs (+0.80 s). Estimated pitch-range changes differed by condition. [5] Two infant-directed TextGrids ended about 0.51 s before their WAVs; we retained all pairs and reported an exclusion sensitivity analysis. A second-tracker diagnostic on 12 recordings exposed pitch sensitivity to parameters and voicing. Measurement audit; pitch diagnostic.

Connecting audibility to listener recognition

R2SPIN supplied the listener outcome absent from Edinburgh: 6,400 binary keyword responses from 16 listeners hearing two recorded speakers under masking. Across eight held-out-list folds, adding the dataset’s keyword glimpse proportion to context predictability improved logistic accuracy from 69.14% to 72.88% and Brier score from 0.2060 to 0.1805 (lower is better). Scaling and fitting stayed within training folds. [6]

This tests the value of audibility information under masking. Glimpse proportion was supplied by the dataset, not measured by our trainee-speech extractor. Shared listeners, speakers and recurring keywords also limit independence. Together, Edinburgh and R2SPIN show why detecting a production change and predicting listener recognition require different evidence. They do not establish intelligibility as an additional indicator in the existing rubric. Supplementary results and figures.

A feasible next test within the rubric

H-A: access to audio improves supported judgements for one existing rubric criterion. Begin with a small set of permitted episodes, including ambiguous cases, selected independently of detector scores. Preserve surrounding turns and check speaker attribution. The pilot should establish whether the criterion can be rated consistently and how much review time it requires before setting a larger sample size.

Independent clinicians should first judge the transcript and context, then review the audio and record whether any revision follows from recovered words, speaker attribution, timing or an unsupported vocal impression. Retain initial judgements and unresolved disagreement before adjudication. This staged reference makes the influence of audio visible; it does not make the reference an objective truth.

Separate reviewers receive transcript-only or transcript-plus-audio versions, with episodes distributed across conditions and no reviewer seeing both versions of an episode. Hold all other context constant. Compare agreement with both the initial transcript reference and the adjudicated full-evidence reference, reporting uncertainty and unresolved cases. An apparent gain only against the audio-informed reference could reflect shared information rather than better assessment. Inspect the reasons for changed decisions and wrong-speaker errors; do not infer clinical validity from agreement alone. The pilot uses human audio review to establish relevance before testing automated cues.

What the exploratory work contributes

The completed work provides reusable extraction and evaluation pipelines, candidate-event retrieval and measurement diagnostics. Its practical contribution is to clarify what the next study needs: reliable speaker attribution, an outcome matched to the research question and a defensible link to a rubric behaviour. The proposed pilot therefore starts with human review of one criterion to establish where audio helps before selecting automated cues. Appendix E records each study’s methods, units and decisions.

03 · Ang Kang Zhe

Educational feedback after simulation

Educational gap and research objective

The inherited AI assessment identifies team communication behaviours across seven competencies and links each observation to transcript evidence. Part B asks how learners and instructors can use that record to support learning.

The existing worksheet presents team communication assessments across seven competencies but is not specifically organised around the different needs of learners and instructors. Learners reviewing the worksheet need to identify which observations are relevant to their performance, interpret what those observations mean, and determine how they could improve. Similarly, instructors need to review the assessment evidence, identify meaningful team communication behaviours, and decide what to address during their debriefs. While the worksheet provides valuable information about team performance, translating these observations into learning opportunities and debrief plans is largely left to its users.

This presents a potential gap in how assessment information supports learning, rather than a demonstrated weakness in the assessment itself. As this gap was identified through reviewing the worksheet rather than observing its users, its actual impact on learners and instructors remains to be established. Further consultation with these users will help determine whether restructuring the feedback could better support their educational needs.

Part B therefore investigates whether the same AI evidence can be transformed, without changing its judgements, into a learner report supporting understanding, reflection and corrective practice, and an instructor report supporting team debriefing. Three questions follow (RQ-B1–B3, Appendix E). Do the reports preserve assessment fidelity, the source judgement and its evidence, and make defensible attribution decisions about whether an observation supports feedback for an individual, role or team? Compared with the original worksheet presenting identical evidence, do they improve learners’ understanding and practice plans and instructors’ debrief preparation? Does communication improve in later simulations? Consultant assessments are reference material, and the generator receives no Part A predictions.

Research foundation and design choices

Three publications guide the design. Hattie and Timperley frame feedback around the learning goal, current performance and next steps. This motivates linking an expected behaviour to the observed exchange and a specific practice goal. [7] Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick emphasise self-assessment, dialogue and opportunities to act on feedback. This motivates an episode-specific reflection question and evidence-based discussion prompts. [8] Alnazawi and Almarhabi’s 2026 scoping review of seven studies found AI supporting communication analysis, facilitators and learner reflection, usually alongside human debriefing, with evidence concentrated on feasibility, accuracy, usability and perceived value rather than objective learning outcomes. [10] These sources justify investigating the proposed features, not assuming that they improve learning.

Proposed reports and initial prototype

Figure 2 illustrates the intended reports using the synthetic request “Could someone prepare the equipment?” It is design content, not a clinical transcript or generated v0.1 output. Routine generation is intended to use AI assessments. Appendix D documents the supplied v0.1 output through aggregate inspection and synthetic illustrations.

Version 0.1 is a working Python prototype that reads supported assessment workbooks and produces learner and instructor drafts, source records and an audit template. It is deterministic: fixed rules and templates produce identical output from identical input, without a language model. Displayed observations keep their cell reference and nearby dialogue. Learner drafts group observations by competency; instructor drafts add counts and selected excerpts. It does not yet assign observations to individuals, interpret complete episodes or generate the guidance in Figure 2. An optional external-model route was not evaluated. Frozen implementation.

What the first prototype shows

The central finding is the difference between including feedback sections and giving guidance that responds to a particular observation. Table 2 records preliminary developer review; independent expert scoring and user testing remain outstanding.

In the separately supplied CT output pair, 30 annotations were reported and 12 displayed in each draft. All 12 learner entries repeat a generic practice instruction. The learner must still work out what to change. The instructor draft likewise organises evidence without a contextualised debrief plan. Appendix D records this output inspection separately from the workbook tests.

Engineering tests provide a bounded starting point. Across 21 workbooks (one AI, 20 CT/NN consultant sources), the generator extracted 371 populated annotation cells with zero mismatches against an independent count and text comparison under recognised layouts, both assuming first-row headers. Three report pairs regenerated identically. These are cells, not validated communication events: the results show extraction and generation feasibility. Appendix C records remaining issues: all 40 AI positive/negative labels unverified, 14/40 AI observations displayed by default, an unsupported header spelling, and five synthetic characterisation tests. Because validity concerns a specific interpretation and use, the reports must represent the assessment faithfully before educational testing. [13]

Evaluation and next steps

Development follows overlapping priorities: faithful assessment, relevant recipient and context, actionable learning, then comparative evaluation, with testing throughout. Source checks on additional permitted AI worksheets will confirm that each observation’s category, positive/negative judgement, evidence and context are preserved. Attribution checks will decide whether it supports individual, role or team feedback. Clinical reviewers will judge whether explanations, questions, actions and debrief prompts suit the behaviour. The frozen v0.1 remains the comparison record.

To evaluate the proposed approach, we plan to compare the theory-guided reports with the existing Excel assessment using identical evidence. The study will investigate whether the redesigned reports improve learners’ understanding, the quality of their corrective practice plans and instructors’ preparation for debriefing. Practice and debrief plans would be independently rated for specificity, appropriateness and evidence support; time and preference are secondary. A generic summary is an optional further comparator.

A pilot will refine cases, scoring criteria, rater agreement and recruitment before sample size is justified. Participants would not see one episode in multiple formats, raters would be blinded to format where feasible, and analysis would account for repeated cases, participants and teams (Appendix B). Later communication performance requires a separate approved study.

Access to AI workbooks, clinical reviewers, participants and ethics and privacy approvals will determine feasible scope. If human testing is delayed, engineering and expert review can continue, leaving learning effectiveness unanswered.

04 · Synthesis and plan

Evidence before integration

Both investigations locate a gap between an output and its intended use: an acoustic prediction needs criterion relevance, and a populated report needs faithful interpretation and educational value. The speech studies identify which measurements can be reproduced and where prediction or attribution breaks down. The feedback prototype shows that accurate copying can coexist with incomplete coverage and generic guidance. These findings define the next tests more precisely. Integration should follow evidence that each stage works for its intended purpose.

The supervisor and clinical partners must confirm the first rubric criterion, independent reviewers and applicable data-access/ethics arrangements. Raw clinical recordings, identifiable workbooks and restricted reading data remain private. The proposed studies depend on these approvals and access arrangements.
