1. Shared context and problem

VR-405 investigates speech analysis and educational feedback in multi-user paediatric resuscitation training. The upstream research describes timestamped, speaker-attributed transcripts and discourse-linked assessment under seven clinically reviewed communication categories. Its evidence-linked worksheet is the starting point for two separate capstone contributions. The supplied manuscript has unconfirmed publication status. [P1]

Charlie investigates whether acoustic evidence can improve interpretation within that existing rubric. Kang Zhe investigates how assessment evidence can support learner reflection and instructor debriefing. Both require context, defensible attribution and independent review. Part B’s current prototype consumes the existing assessment workbook; it does not consume Part A predictions. The interim submission draft contains the newer authored Part B.

Part A · Charlie Hee Ming Guan · Speech analysis

Acoustic evidence within the existing rubric

Research question: Can correctly speaker-attributed acoustic evidence improve identification or interpretation of behaviours within the existing seven-category communication rubric, compared with transcript-only review? This proposed comparison follows interim examiner feedback; improvement and supervisor approval of the narrowed scope remain unestablished.

The initial proposal added Professionalism and Confidence columns. The examiner questioned the justification for new constructs and their overlap with established categories. We have set that design aside. The seven categories remain Roles & Responsibilities, Closed-Loop Communication, Clear Messages, Knowing One’s Limitations, Constructive Intervention, Summarisation & Re-evaluation, and Knowledge Sharing. The reported historical reduction from 12 constructs to seven requires an original source; its selection procedure is not inferred here. [P1]

Informal listening highlighted laughter during a positively assessed summary and hesitant-sounding delivery during a recognised self-correction. These observations motivate checking whether audio supplies relevant evidence; neither establishes an incorrect rubric decision or its prevalence. A laugh can have different interpersonal meanings, while perceived certainty does not establish correctness or competence.

Choosing a defensible assessment target

Clear Messages is a candidate because delivery may affect reception, but its supplied criteria concern directedness, specificity and brevity. Intelligibility is not automatically a formal indicator. Clinicians must confirm whether a particular audio observation helps apply an existing criterion without changing its meaning. Perceived confidence cannot be mapped directly to Knowing One’s Limitations. Laughter detection can locate material for review but cannot assign professionalism.

Completed confidence and event prototypes

The reading-confidence pipeline processed 600 externally supplied, teacher-labelled children’s reading recordings using 88 eGeMAPSv02 functionals plus duration and three RMS descriptors. Five-fold recording-level evaluation gave macro-F1 0.7849 for Random Forest, 0.7104 for logistic regression and 0.1916 for the majority baseline. Imputation and logistic scaling were fitted within training folds. Speakers and texts may overlap across folds; authoritative speaker metadata remain unresolved. These are label-recovery results, not clinical or unseen-speaker validation. [3] [methods and aggregates]

The saved logistic model powers the private demonstration; the Forest result does not describe that demo. Applied to VR recordings, outputs were not dependable under spontaneous adult speech, silence, microphone bleed and missing linguistic context. The four added descriptors do not alone explain benchmark performance, but the fixed-fold ablations cannot remove speaker, text or recording confounds. Duration is not speaking rate and digital RMS is not calibrated vocal intensity. [ablations]

The pretrained YAMNet event pipeline processed eight original WAV channels and returned 19 candidate intervals. Channels overlap acoustically, so these are not 19 independent episodes. Informal listening found plausible examples; there is no exhaustive human reference or precision/recall estimate. Scores are uncalibrated event outputs. Possible missed events remain unadjudicated. [source audit]

Intelligibility groundwork and its limits

Edinburgh contains 125 recordings from one speaker: 25 matched sentences across five speaking conditions. Annotation-based spans increased relative to plain speech in all 25 computer-directed pairs (median +1.73 s) and all 25 non-native-listener-directed pairs (+0.80 s). Estimated pitch-range changes differed by condition. This establishes descriptive production contrasts, not improved listener recognition. Two infant-directed TextGrids under-cover their WAVs; measurement and pitch limitations remain documented. No independent recognition labels were available in this analysis. [10] [measurement audit]

Human-recognition precursor

R2SPIN supplied 6,400 keyword-recognition observations from 16 listeners and two recorded speakers. In eight held-out-list folds, context-only logistic accuracy/Brier was 69.14%/0.2060; adding published keyword glimpse proportion gave 72.88%/0.1805. This predictor describes acoustic audibility under masking, not a feature estimated from our trainee speech. Listeners, speakers and some keywords overlap across folds, so rows are not independent replication. Neither R2SPIN nor Edinburgh validates speaker-side prediction of clinical intelligibility or Clear Messages. [11] [fold results]

Proposed comparison and evidence gates

The next experiment should compare interpretations of the same episodes under transcript-only and transcript-plus-audio conditions against independent clinical annotations using the unchanged rubric. Prespecify one criterion, episode selection, exclusions and decision rules. Separate reference annotators from condition reviewers; counterbalance cases or use separate reviewer groups to reduce recall. Record disagreement before adjudication and retain uncertain cases rather than manufacture ground truth.

Report category-level precision, recall and F1 against the clinical reference, inter-rater agreement, wrong-speaker errors and whether audio changes a defensible decision. Account for repeated episodes within teams and reviewers. A feasibility pilot must establish available independent cases, annotation burden and disagreement before sample-size or precision claims. If clinicians cannot participate, complete attribution and software audits while deferring rubric-improvement claims.

Interpretation and next decisions

The main limitation is construct validity: a measurable signal has no automatic clinical meaning. Cross-talk, uncertain speaker attribution, domain mismatch and informal listening constrain the prototypes. No improved seven-category accuracy, educational benefit or integrated audio-driven feedback has been demonstrated. Charlie owns criterion selection, audio curation and comparison planning with clinical reviewers; Kang Zhe’s feedback work remains separate. New annotation depends on confirmed access, consent and applicable approvals. The supervisor must approve the first criterion, annotation feasibility and a useful success measure. Part B · Ang Kang Zhe · Feedback presentation and debriefing

8. Research objective and contribution

Automated assessment identifies communication behaviours; educational feedback must also help recipients interpret those observations and decide how to improve. The existing paediatric VR research pipeline produces rubric-aligned, evidence-linked worksheets covering seven communication competencies. This contribution investigates how those assessments can support individual learning and instructor-led team debriefing.

The proposed contribution is a theory-guided transformation framework that preserves assessment evidence while making the expected behaviour, observed performance and next practice action explicit. Its intended value is to improve learners’ comprehension of expected and observed behaviour and the quality of corrective learning plans, and to reduce instructors’ effort in preparing an evidence-based debrief. Role relevance refers to the recipient’s observed actions and responsibilities, with team context retained.

The research contribution lies in specifying and evaluating the transformation rules: evidence eligibility, recipient attribution, educational content and links to source material. These rules make the method reproducible and distinguish it from an unconstrained narrative summary. Whether they improve learning or debrief preparation remains to be established.

RQ-B1 — Feedback fidelity and quality: Can learner- and instructor-facing feedback preserve assessment evidence, meaning, category/polarity and defensible individual/team attribution? How clear, relevant and actionable is the output when assessed separately? RQ-B2 — Immediate educational usefulness: Compared with generic feedback from identical evidence, does theory-guided feedback improve learner comprehension, corrective learning-plan quality and instructors’ preparation of accurate, actionable debriefs? RQ-B3 — Subsequent learning effectiveness: Does theory-guided feedback improve communication performance in subsequent simulation scenarios? RQ-B1 has partial mechanical evidence; semantic fidelity, feedback quality and RQ-B2/B3 remain unestablished. The original assessments and expert annotations belong to the upstream research team.

9. Educational framework and design rationale

Hattie and Timperley organise feedback around goals, current performance and subsequent action. [18] Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick emphasise self-assessment, dialogue and opportunities to use feedback in later work. [19] The proposed framework combines these perspectives into an evidence-linked sequence of criterion, observation, reflection and practice action (Table 4).

Table 4. Educational principles, design decisions and measurable outcomes.
PrincipleDesign decisionProposed outcome
Goals and performance information [18]Pair an approved behavioural criterion with the observed episode and source reference.Comprehension of expected and observed behaviour, including recognition of performance gaps.
Next-step information [18]Separate a proposed practice action from the original observation.Expert-rated specificity and appropriateness of corrective learning plans.
Self-assessment and dialogue [19]Provide learner reflection and instructor discussion prompts from the same evidence.Interpretation accuracy and debrief preparation quality.
Opportunity to close the gap [19]Carry a learner-selected practice goal into a later scenario.Subsequent behaviour under comparable task opportunities.

Post-simulation delivery permits review of complete exchanges and supports facilitated reflection. PEARLS provides a simulation-specific precedent for combining self-assessment, discussion and teaching. [20] In a 161-student study, post-simulation debriefing received higher self-reported learning ratings than in-simulation debriefing; objective transfer was not established. [21] Timing is therefore a contextual design choice, rather than an assumed universal advantage.

The existing worksheet retains detailed annotations but requires recipients to derive their own learning priorities. Generic LLM summaries offer rapid synthesis, although source fidelity and attribution depend on generation behaviour. A structured transformation makes those decisions inspectable and permits matched-evidence comparison. The selected representation is a concise summary with access to the underlying episode; its effectiveness requires testing.

10. Feedback generator v0.1

The implemented product is a Python workbook-to-feedback generator. It reads recognised competency columns, retains each populated assessment cell and its source pointer, collects nearby dialogue, and produces learner/instructor Markdown drafts, source-record JSON and an audit template. The frozen v0.1 source and reproduction instructions identify the exact implementation tested. Appendix D inspects a supplied learner/instructor output pair, separating visible report structure from unverified meaning and educational quality.

Local generation applies fixed competency targets, reflection questions and practice/debrief templates. The learner output is organised by competency, not reliably by individual. Instructor output provides annotation counts and selected excerpts, not validated team scores or prioritised synthesis. Attribution remains team-level or unverified. All seven output headings are retained, but input-header recognition and selected-example coverage require separate checking.

The local mode makes structure and provenance reproducible while exposing whether generic templates satisfy the intended educational requirements. An optional external-model branch exists but was not executed; its model availability, fidelity and 30-record submission limit remain untested. Neither prototype execution nor template labels establish educational benefit. The earlier content concepts in Appendix B remain design references rather than validated output examples.

11. Evaluation and preliminary findings

Evaluation separates technical/content fidelity, feedback quality and educational outcomes. Validity concerns the proposed interpretation and use, not merely software operation. [23] Feedback literacy further distinguishes understanding and planning from actual uptake. [22] These domains form a project-specific evaluation design, not a validated composite scale.

11.1 Product-focused evaluation and preliminary evidence

The object of evaluation is the generated learner/instructor feedback: whether it preserves the assessment, implements the educational structure and supports reflection, practice planning and debriefing. Table 5 relates these requirements to the available evidence. Its qualitative findings are developer observations from the implementation and outputs, not independent expert ratings or user-test results.

Supporting engineering checks covered extraction from 21 workbooks (371 populated annotation cells), three generated report pairs and repeated local runs. Cell counts and text matched for the recognised layouts, but both extraction checks assume headers occur in the first row. These results support technical feasibility; they do not establish faithful interpretation or educational quality. Appendix C documents the engineering protocol, synthetic regressions and aggregates. No external AI calls or participant study were conducted.

11.2 Analysis of fidelity and feedback quality

Count agreement supports extraction for the tested layouts but cannot establish correct polarity, complete event context or defensible individual attribution. The unmapped colours explain why the AI source loses usable polarity despite exact annotation copying. The display cap explains why preserved source records do not imply complete report coverage. A synthetic test also found that the internal label “Summarisation & Re-evaluation” is not accepted as an input header, exposing a silent-exclusion risk beyond the supplied layouts.

Theory-derived fields are present, but reflection and feed-forward are repeated generic templates rather than episode-specific guidance. Instructor counts offer organisation without establishing prevalence or teaching priority. This is a developer code/output analysis, not independent expert quality ratings. Clarity, relevance, specificity and actionability require separate review; the conversation-based Feedback Quality Instrument is a candidate source of domains, not a validated static-report measure. [24] Digital-feedback instrument evidence remains limited. [26]

11.3 Educational evaluation still required

After fidelity correction and clinical review, compare generic and theory-guided outputs from identical evidence. Learner outcomes are feedback comprehension, including gap recognition, and corrective learning-plan quality. Instructor outcomes are accurate, useful and actionable debrief preparation; time is secondary. Planned elements include counterbalanced cases, prespecified anchors, blinded ratings where feasible and pilot-based sample-size justification.

Later communication performance requires independently assessed, comparable simulations. Kirkpatrick distinguishes learning, workplace behaviour and downstream results. [25] [27] The upstream balance rate B = (G − R)/(G + R) remains a candidate: it is undefined at G + R = 0 and sensitive to event definitions, annotation practices, team dependence and behavioural opportunities. No comprehension, uptake, learning-effectiveness, retention, transfer or patient-benefit result is available for v0.1.

12. Observed limitations and development plan

Observed shortcomings are unmapped colours, silent header exclusion, unreported selection omissions, limited adjacent-row context, generic actions and absent individual routing. Workbook handles also produce Windows resource warnings. These findings define engineering priorities; clinical appropriateness, user comprehension and educational effects remain untested risks. The current output is an initial functional prototype, not a validated final product.

13. Shared outlook

The completed speech prototypes establish bounded reading-label, event-candidate and production/audibility results. The next question is whether attributed audio improves an existing rubric decision. The separate feedback prototype establishes workbook extraction and report generation with unresolved fidelity and educational questions. Clinical review and independent evaluation are required before either contribution supports stronger claims.
