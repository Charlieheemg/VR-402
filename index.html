1. Project context and problem

The shared context is paediatric VR training: using evidence from a simulated team interaction to support performance assessment and post-simulation debriefing. This project explores both what the assessment can recover from recorded speech and how its findings can help trainees and instructors reflect on performance.

Plain transcripts preserve words, potentially including fillers, but not the original pitch, intensity or detailed timing. Experiments on vocal confidence show that these prosodic cues contribute to listeners’ impressions alongside linguistic content (Jiang & Pell, 2017). This motivates testing whether retaining audio evidence improves assessment beyond transcript/context alone; benefits for paediatric debriefing remain to be evaluated.

3. Related work and evidence boundaries

Delivery cues and their limits. Response delays, filled pauses and intonation influenced listeners’ estimates of a speaker’s knowledge, with effects depending on whether a response was an answer or a nonanswer (Brennan & Williams, 1995). In a controlled expressive-speech study, unconfident expressions had slower speaking rates and more pauses; pitch and amplitude/intensity patterns also differentiated confidence levels (Jiang & Pell, 2017). These findings motivate candidate features, not universal thresholds or a clinical competence score.

Measurement versus inference. A pause duration is a measurement; hesitation is an interpretation; its cause is a further claim. Perceived confidence (listener ratings), self-reported certainty (speaker ratings), and correctness (task scoring) are distinct targets: Pon-Barry and Shieber annotated all three and found that perceived and self-reported certainty often differed (2010). We will keep these labels separate and check automated measurements against human annotations.

Explicit acoustic evidence. openSMILE is a signal-analysis and feature-extraction toolkit (Eyben et al., 2010); eGeMAPS is a compact, standardised acoustic feature set covering frequency, energy and spectral descriptors (Eyben et al., 2016). These provide interpretable numerical acoustic measurements, not semantic conclusions about confidence or teamwork. Connecting measurements to an assessment rubric requires a separate, validated interpretation stage.

Assessment precedent and data. Naim et al. combined language, prosody and facial features to predict observer ratings in 138 mock interviews from 69 participants (2015). This offers a performance-rating and annotation precedent, but neither its dataset nor its results establish validity for paediatric teamwork.

Practical design precedent. The NUS-hosted VMock guide separates speech delivery, content strength and non-verbal feedback, with replay and time-aligned feedback (VMock, n.d.). It motivates separating delivery from content in our interface; product documentation does not establish the validity of proprietary acoustic scores or reveal the deployed algorithm.

Internal aviation related work. From Roleplay to Virtual Reality provides an adaptive cabin-crew training example (supplied manuscript, n.d.); Signals of Aggression examines multimodal cues and perceived aggression in virtual agents (Ong et al., 2026). Both inform internal discussion of training and perception, not validation of paediatric assessment. The supplied files are not reproduced here.

4. Research question and workstreams

Shared question: How can richer assessment evidence and clearer feedback improve AI-supported debriefing for paediatric VR training?

  • Charlie Hee — richer assessment from raw speech audio: investigate whether information lost during STT improves assessment beyond transcript and conversational context. The scope is exploratory, rather than a predetermined “confidence detector”.
  • Ang Kang Zhe — presentation of analysis and feedback: investigate how findings, supporting evidence and uncertainty can be organised and presented to support debriefing and learning.

The proposed integration connects interpretable evidence and assessment outputs to Kang Zhe’s feedback interface. Each workstream retains its own evaluation: assessment validity and feedback usefulness.

5. Proposed methodology

Compare three conditions on the same recorded interactions, with matched assessment units, rubric and scenario context:

  1. A · Current transcript/context baseline. Reproduce the verified current pipeline and document its model, prompts and inputs. The provisional assumption about timestamps must be checked before comparison.
  2. B · Engineered audio-feature pathway. Extract timing/disfluency evidence, such as pauses, speech rate and fillers, using suitable segmentation, alignment and detection methods, alongside openSMILE/eGeMAPS acoustic measurements (Eyben et al., 2010; Eyben et al., 2016). Convert selected values into structured text/JSON with units, time spans and measurement caveats. Give this evidence plus the transcript/context to a language model for rubric-based interpretation. openSMILE alone does not supply all timing or filler annotations.
  3. C · Direct raw-audio assessment. Give the original recording and matched task context to an audio-capable language model for assessment. SALMONN demonstrates an architecture connecting speech/audio encoders to an LLM for audio understanding (Tang et al., 2024). Such capabilities motivate this alternative; general audio benchmarks do not establish paediatric assessment validity.

Implementation candidate. OpenAI GPT-Audio-1.5 (gpt-audio-1.5) accepts audio through the Chat Completions API and is one concrete candidate for direct raw-audio analysis (OpenAI documentation). The methodology remains model-agnostic: other suitable audio-language models may be evaluated based on accessibility, cost, latency, domain suitability and performance.

Independent human reference. Have raters assess the same recordings using an agreed rubric, independently of model outputs. Report inter-rater agreement and disagreements; human ratings are a reference with uncertainty. For approximately continuous ratings, specify an appropriate intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) form and confidence interval (Koo & Li, 2016); choose an agreement measure suited to the scale for ordinal labels. Keep listener-rated delivery, any collected self-reported certainty, and procedural correctness separate (Pon-Barry & Shieber, 2010).

First check measurement accuracy against manually annotated examples, then compare assessment agreement and evidence quality across A–C. Keep the text model and prompt consistent between A and B where feasible, separate development and evaluation speakers, and record model versions and settings. Consider recording conditions, language/accent variation and speaker normalization before interpreting acoustic differences.

An optional later hybrid may combine engineered evidence with direct audio assessment, if the initial comparison justifies the extra complexity. Improved model agreement alone would not demonstrate improved learning; feedback usefulness needs a separate evaluation with Kang Zhe’s interface.

6. Implementation and evaluation status

This report defines a proposed methodology. No new prototype, collected human ratings, comparative scores or learning outcomes are reported. The next deliverable is a small, auditable comparison on approved recordings, with time-linked evidence and clearly labelled interpretations.
