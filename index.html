Interim findings

From recorded communication to useful feedback

This project investigates two stages of communication feedback in paediatric virtual-reality training. Charlie examines whether acoustic evidence can help interpret behaviours within an established seven-category rubric. Kang Zhe examines how existing assessment worksheets can become useful learner and instructor reports. Speech experiments demonstrate reproducible acoustic measurement and prediction on external datasets, while clinical demonstrations expose attribution and interpretation problems. A deterministic feedback prototype establishes repeatable extraction and report generation while exposing gaps in coverage, interpretation and educational specificity. Together, the findings identify what must be verified before additional evidence or redesigned feedback can support training. The prototypes remain separate; neither has demonstrated improved clinical communication or learning.

01 · Shared context

Problem and contribution

Paediatric resuscitation requires teams to allocate roles, exchange instructions and update a shared understanding under time pressure. In a hypothetical equipment request, a transcript may preserve the words while omitting delivery and overlapping speech; a worksheet may identify a communication problem while leaving trainees unsure how to improve. These are distinct gaps between recorded speech, assessment and learning.

VR-405 builds on the NUS Immersive Reality Lab’s multi-user simulation and prior clinical collaboration. The upstream research team developed speaker-attributed transcription, discourse-thread reconstruction and evidence-linked positive/negative assessment worksheets. Its manuscript describes seven categories refined with clinical subject-matter experts: Roles & Responsibilities, Closed Loop Communication, Clear Messages, Knowing One’s Limitations, Constructive Intervention, Summarisation & Re-evaluation, and Knowledge Sharing. These categories and the upstream system are inherited work, not capstone inventions. [P1]

The capstone addresses two questions the existing assessment does not settle: whether vocal evidence helps interpret an established behaviour, and whether a different presentation helps readers use the assessment. Published paediatric VR research supports investigating debriefing in this setting, but does not validate our methods. Sohlin et al.’s 24-professional pilot found the training feasible; a larger study was needed to evaluate non-inferiority between debriefing methods. [1]

Value and intended deliverables. The contribution is a testable extension of an existing workflow: an auditable account of when audio changes a supported rubric judgement, and feedback formats that connect assessed behaviour to a defensible next action. Deliverables are a bounded speech-evidence protocol and feasibility findings (Charlie), and a traceable report generator with fidelity and usefulness evaluation (Kang Zhe). The research value lies in testing these links in this setting; broader algorithmic novelty is not claimed.

02 · Charlie Hee Ming Guan

Acoustic evidence within the existing rubric

Research gap and scope decision

Listening to selected existing recordings revealed laughter during a positively assessed summary and hesitant-sounding delivery during a recognised self-correction. These informal observations show that delivery can be salient; they do not establish that the original assessment was wrong. Laughter can serve different interpersonal functions, and perceived certainty differs from correctness or competence.

The initial Professionalism and Confidence column proposal was set aside following examiner feedback. The revised question is: does correctly attributed audio improve identification or interpretation of an existing rubric behaviour compared with transcript-only review? The intended contribution is to test the value of additional evidence while keeping the rubric fixed. The reported historical reduction from 12 constructs to seven remains unverified; the available manuscript establishes the seven-category definitions but not that derivation. [P1]

Clear Messages is a candidate, not a confirmed acoustic target. Its existing criteria concern directedness, specificity and brevity, including the referent, action and timing. Intelligibility must not be silently added as an indicator. Likewise, sounding confident cannot directly score Knowing One’s Limitations, which concerns help-seeking, uncertainty disclosure and escalation. Rubric-owner review must establish the relevance of any audio cue before a model output becomes assessment evidence.

This distinction avoids treating a measurable variable as the desired outcome. Clear-speech research also shows that speaking style can change several acoustic dimensions; slowing alone is not a sufficient operational definition. [2] The experiments below therefore examine complementary feasibility questions rather than compete as interchangeable clinical scores.

Implemented speech investigations

The work spans feature extraction, confidence/event demonstrations, and production and perception studies. These investigations now inform a narrower rubric-based question. These completed exploratory studies test components and expose failure modes; they were not designed as confirmatory tests of the revised hypothesis. Appendix E maps each question to its experimental unit, procedure, outcome and subsequent decision.

Reading confidence. The pipeline extracted 88 eGeMAPSv02 acoustic summaries, covering properties such as pitch and voice quality, plus duration and three root-mean-square (RMS) amplitude descriptors from 600 teacher-labelled children’s reading recordings. Fixed five-fold stratified recording-level evaluation compared a majority baseline, logistic regression and Random Forest. Imputation and logistic scaling were fitted within each training fold. Pooled macro-F1 was 0.1916 for the majority baseline, 0.7104 for logistic regression and 0.7849 for Random Forest. Macro-F1 gives each class equal weight by averaging its F1 score. Both learned models recovered rating information beyond the majority baseline under this evaluation design. [3] [4] Methods and aggregates.

Speaker independence could not be established from authoritative metadata. Speakers, text and recording conditions may overlap across folds. Feature ablations show that the four added descriptors alone do not explain performance, without eliminating those confounds. The private clinical demo uses the saved logistic model, not the Forest. Qualitative application exposed unstable interpretations under spontaneous adult speech, silence, microphone bleed and absent linguistic context. Digital RMS is not calibrated vocal intensity; duration is not speaking rate without linguistic counts.

Laughter candidates. A pretrained YAMNet pipeline processed eight original WAV channels and returned 19 candidate intervals. Informal listening identified plausible examples, but no exhaustive independent event annotation supports precision or recall. Channels share acoustic content, so intervals are not independent episodes. Model scores are uncalibrated, and uncertain speaker attribution prevents assigning an event to a trainee solely from the microphone label. This prototype can surface audio for review; it cannot score professionalism. Aggregate provenance and limitations.

Perceived emotional delivery. A separate completed CREMA-D experiment used 720 acted recordings from 30 speakers and four sentences. Logistic regression on 88 eGeMAPS features predicted the emotion receiving the most audio-only listener votes, with 20/5/5 speakers separated for training, validation and test. Sixty-two tied recordings were excluded from single-label scoring. On 113 scored test clips, macro-F1 was 0.367 versus 0.117 for the majority baseline; training macro-F1 was 0.886. The large training–test gap indicates limited transfer to new speakers. Speaker separation addresses an unresolved weakness of the reading-confidence benchmark, but the different targets and datasets prevent direct comparison of their F1 scores. Neither task validates clinical communication judgements. Pinned methods, results and demonstration limits.

Intelligibility groundwork

Production contrasts. Edinburgh supplied 125 recordings: one speaker, 25 matched sentences and five conditions. First-to-last non-pure-silence annotation spans increased relative to plain speech in all 25 computer-directed pairs (median +1.73 s) and all 25 non-native-listener-directed pairs (+0.80 s). Estimated pitch-range changes differed by condition, arguing against one universal acoustic direction. These are within-speaker production findings, not listener-recognition gains. [5]

Two infant-directed TextGrids end about 0.51 s before their WAVs. Retaining all pairs and reporting a separate exclusion sensitivity analysis preserves the anomaly transparently. A 12-recording comparison with a second pitch tracker also identified parameter and voicing sensitivity; agreement between algorithms is not ground truth. The corpus analysis lacks independent recognition labels. Measurement audit; pitch diagnostic.

Predicting keyword recognition under masking

R2SPIN supplied 6,400 binary keyword-recognition observations from 16 listeners and two recorded speakers. Logistic models evaluated across eight held-out-list folds gave accuracy/Brier of 69.14%/0.2060 using context predictability and 72.88%/0.1805 after adding published keyword glimpse proportion. Lower Brier indicates less squared probability error. Scaling and fitting remained within training folds. [6]

This demonstrates predictive value of audibility under the original masking conditions. Keyword glimpse proportion was supplied by the dataset, not extracted from trainee speech. Shared listeners, speakers and recurring keywords prevent treating rows as independent replication. Neither this association nor Edinburgh validates speaker-side prediction of clinical intelligibility. Results, figures and reproduction.

Next test and decision rule

H-A (proposed): audio improves agreement with an independent clinical reference for one prespecified existing criterion. After rubric-owner confirmation, curate approved communication episodes with transcripts, timing and verified speaker/role attribution. Preserve enough surrounding turns to interpret the behaviour, and flag uncertain cross-talk for review or exclusion rather than create a new rubric category. Preselect episodes independently of detector scores and include ambiguous cases. Independent clinicians should review the permitted episode evidence and record criterion judgements, reasons and disagreement before adjudication.

Compare transcript-only and transcript-plus-audio review of the same episodes, holding transcript and contextual information constant. Separate reference annotators from condition reviewers; allocate cases so reviewers do not first see one version and then recall it in the other condition. The primary outcome is the paired change in agreement with the clinical reference; criterion-specific precision/recall, wrong-speaker errors and reviewer usefulness explain that change. Account for clustering by team, episode and reviewer. Pilot annotation burden and disagreement before justifying sample size or inferential claims. If audio produces more flags without better-supported decisions, that is evidence against its proposed use.

Interpretation

Completed work establishes acoustic extraction, bounded prediction and event-review feasibility. Its central lesson is that signal detection, listener perception and clinical interpretation require different evidence. Speaker attribution and construct relevance are now prerequisites for further modelling. No seven-category accuracy improvement or training benefit has been demonstrated. The next contribution can be useful even if it identifies which audio cues should be excluded from assessment.

03 · Ang Kang Zhe

Educational feedback after simulation

Educational gap and research objective

The inherited AI assessment identifies team communication behaviours across seven competencies and links each observation to transcript evidence. Part B asks how learners and instructors can use that record to support learning.

The existing worksheet presents team communication assessments across seven competencies but is not specifically organised around the different needs of learners and instructors. Learners reviewing the worksheet must identify which observations are relevant to their performance, interpret what those observations mean, and determine how they could improve. Similarly, instructors must review the assessment evidence, identify meaningful team communication behaviours, and decide what to address during their debriefs. While the worksheet provides valuable information about team performance, translating these observations into learning opportunities and debrief plans is largely left to its users.

This presents a potential gap in how assessment information supports learning, rather than a demonstrated weakness in the assessment itself. As this gap was identified through reviewing the worksheet rather than observing its users, its actual impact on learners and instructors remains to be established. Further consultation with these users will help determine whether restructuring the feedback could better support their educational needs.

Part B therefore investigates whether the same AI evidence can be transformed, without changing its judgements, into a learner report supporting understanding, reflection and corrective practice, and an instructor report supporting team debriefing. Three questions follow (RQ-B1–B3, Appendix E). Do the reports preserve assessment fidelity, the source judgement and its evidence, and make defensible attribution decisions about whether an observation supports feedback for an individual, role or team? Compared with the original worksheet presenting identical evidence, do they improve learners’ understanding and practice plans and instructors’ debrief preparation? Does communication improve in later simulations? Consultant assessments are reference material, and the generator receives no Part A predictions.

Research foundation and design choices

Three publications guide the design. Hattie and Timperley frame feedback around the learning goal, current performance and next steps. This motivates linking an expected behaviour to the observed exchange and a specific practice goal, though it prescribes no report layout. [7] Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick emphasise self-assessment, dialogue and opportunities to act on feedback. This motivates an episode-specific reflection question and evidence-based discussion prompts, without showing that a written question will be used. [8] Alnazawi and Almarhabi’s 2026 scoping review of seven studies found AI supporting communication analysis, facilitators and learner reflection, usually alongside human debriefing, with evidence concentrated on feasibility, accuracy, usability and perceived value rather than objective learning outcomes. [10] These sources justify investigating the proposed features, not assuming that they improve learning.

Both reports keep all seven categories and their evidence accessible. Where the record does not support individual responsibility, feedback remains at role or team level, and the instructor selects the teaching emphasis.

Proposed reports and initial prototype

Figure 2 illustrates the intended reports using a redacted consultant observation at Summary!D71, where an oxygen request was flagged for not identifying the receiver or flow rate. The consultant source is only an illustration; routine generation is intended to use AI assessments. The proposed wording requires clinical review; actual v0.1 wording is in Appendix D.

Version 0.1 is a working Python prototype that reads supported assessment workbooks and produces learner and instructor drafts, source records and an audit template. It is deterministic: fixed rules and templates produce identical output from identical input, without a language model. Displayed observations keep their cell reference and nearby dialogue. Learner drafts group observations by competency; instructor drafts add counts and selected excerpts. It does not yet assign observations to individuals, interpret complete episodes or generate the guidance in Figure 2. An optional external-model route was not evaluated. Frozen implementation.

What the first prototype shows

The central finding is the difference between including feedback sections and giving guidance that responds to a particular observation. Table 3 records preliminary developer review; independent expert scoring and user testing remain outstanding.

In the oxygen example, the actual draft asks generally how the team’s action compares with the expected behaviour and advises rehearsing “the target behaviour”, the instruction given for every displayed observation. The learner must still work out what to change. The instructor draft likewise organises evidence without a contextualised debrief plan.

Engineering tests provide a bounded starting point. Across 21 workbooks (one AI, 20 CT/NN consultant sources), the generator extracted 371 populated annotation cells with zero mismatches against an independent count and text comparison under recognised layouts, both assuming first-row headers. Three report pairs regenerated identically. These are cells, not validated communication events: the results show extraction and generation feasibility. Appendix C records remaining issues: all 40 AI positive/negative labels unverified, 14/40 AI observations displayed by default, an unsupported header spelling, and five synthetic characterisation tests. Because validity concerns a specific interpretation and use, the reports must represent the assessment faithfully before educational testing. [13]

Evaluation and next steps

Development follows overlapping priorities: faithful assessment, relevant recipient and context, actionable learning, then comparative evaluation, with testing throughout. Source checks on additional permitted AI worksheets will confirm that each observation’s category, positive/negative judgement, evidence and context are preserved. Attribution checks will decide whether it supports individual, role or team feedback. Clinical reviewers will judge whether explanations, questions, actions and debrief prompts suit the behaviour. The frozen v0.1 remains the comparison record.

To evaluate the proposed approach, we plan to compare the theory-guided reports with the existing Excel assessment using identical evidence. The study will investigate whether the redesigned reports improve learners’ understanding, the quality of their corrective practice plans and instructors’ preparation for debriefing. Practice and debrief plans would be independently rated for specificity, appropriateness and evidence support; time and preference are secondary. A generic summary is an optional further comparator.

A pilot will refine cases, scoring criteria, rater agreement and recruitment before sample size is justified. Participants would not see one episode in multiple formats, raters would be blinded to format where feasible, and analysis would account for repeated cases, participants and teams (Appendix B). Later communication performance requires a separate approved study. No educational benefit has yet been demonstrated.

Access to AI workbooks, clinical reviewers, participants and ethics and privacy approvals will determine feasible scope. If human testing is delayed, engineering and expert review can continue, leaving learning effectiveness unanswered.

04 · Synthesis and plan

Evidence before integration

Both investigations locate a gap between an output and its intended use: an acoustic prediction needs criterion relevance, and a populated report needs faithful interpretation and educational value. The speech studies identify which measurements can be reproduced and where prediction or attribution breaks down. The feedback prototype shows that accurate copying can coexist with incomplete coverage and generic guidance. These findings define the next tests more precisely. Integration should follow evidence that each stage works for its intended purpose.

The research design and contingency matrix specifies outputs, dependencies and stopping rules. If piloting does not support reliable labels, feasible recruitment or defensible comparison, Weeks 7–8 become protocol refinement rather than an underpowered effectiveness claim.

The supervisor and clinical partners must confirm the first rubric criterion, independent reviewers and applicable data-access/ethics arrangements. Raw clinical recordings, identifiable workbooks and restricted reading data remain private. Approvals and data access are prerequisites for the proposed studies; no prospective clinical or educational evaluation is claimed.
